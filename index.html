Know before you go

What conditions lead to a black hole forming, and how does its gravity affect its surroundings?

Black holes begin their existence as stars. As soon as the star exhausts its fuel, the star collapses in on itself. And that produces a great explosion known as a supernova. Provided that the core remaining after that is sufficiently massive, there is no force capable of preventing the collapse of the core into a black hole. A star needs about 20 times the Sun's mass to eventually leave one behind, and the leftover core itself must still be about 3 times the Sun's mass or more for the collapse to happen. The mass condenses into a very small volume; therefore, its gravitational pull is quite significant. Once an object passes the threshold point referred to as the event horizon, neither matter nor energy can escape it. The falling gas and dust heat up and emit light; thus, creating a glowing disk. Getting too close will result in your being stretched like a piece of taffy, and time itself appears to slow down the nearer you get to that edge. The closest known black hole to Earth sits about 1,500 light-years away, while supermassive ones, far larger, anchor the center of most big galaxies, ours included. The very first black hole ever photographed, known as M87*, wasn't captured on camera until 2019.

What makes an exoplanet potentially habitable?

An exoplanet is just a planet outside our solar system. We have found more than 6,000 so far. A planet has a shot at life if it sits in its star's "just right" zone — not too hot, not too cold — so water can stay wet instead of turning to ice or steam, and liquid water is really the main sign scientists look for when judging habitability. Most exoplanets are found by watching a star dim a tiny bit as a planet passes in front of it. Many sit close to small, cool stars, so their skies and light can look very different from ours; rocky, Earth-sized planets turn out to be especially common around these red dwarfs. The closest possibly habitable exoplanet we know of, Proxima b, is only 4.2 light-years away. Kepler-186f made history as the first Earth-sized planet ever found in a habitable zone, and the TRAPPIST-1 system takes it further with seven Earth-sized planets circling a single small star. One catch is that many habitable-zone planets end up tidally locked, meaning the same side always faces the star while the other stays in permanent night. Some may have water. None are proven to have life yet. That is part of why people keep looking.
